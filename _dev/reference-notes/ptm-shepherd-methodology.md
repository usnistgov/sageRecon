PTM-Shepherd’s mass‑shift clustering takes an open‑search PSM list, builds a global delta‑mass histogram, calls prominent peaks, and then characterizes each peak by localization, spectral similarity, and retention‑time (RT) shift to distinguish true PTMs from artifacts and adducts. The formaldehyde (+30.01 Da) example in FFPE data illustrates how weak localization plus minimal RT and spectral effects point to a non‑covalent adduct rather than a covalent PTM. [ptmshepherd.nesvilab](https://ptmshepherd.nesvilab.org)

## From delta‑mass histogram to peaks

PTM‑Shepherd first aggregates all precursor mass shifts (open‑search delta masses) across one or more experiments into a single histogram, typically with fine binning (e.g. 0.0002–0.001 Da depending on configuration). Each bin counts the number of PSMs whose MS1 mass shift falls into that range, giving a 1D density of observed modifications and artifacts. Peaks in this histogram are treated as **mass‑shift clusters**, each corresponding to one candidate chemical event (PTM, adduct, isotope, insertion/deletion, etc.). [github](https://github.com/Nesvilab/PTM-Shepherd)

Peak picking is based on a combination of local signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) and prominence. SNR is computed for each candidate apex using a sliding window (~0.004 Da by default) against flanking background windows (~0.005 Da on each side, scaled with the peak‑picking width), which penalizes noisy regions of the histogram. Prominence is defined as the ratio of the apex intensity to the more intense of its left or right shoulder, where shoulders are found by following the histogram downward monotonically until a local minimum is reached. A peak is accepted when its prominence exceeds a default threshold (0.3) and its SNR is sufficiently high; from all accepted peaks, the top 500 by intensity are passed downstream for characterization. [github](https://github.com/Nesvilab/PTM-Shepherd/blob/main/README.md)

For each accepted peak, PTM‑Shepherd defines boundaries either at the observed shoulders or at the precursor tolerance limit, whichever is closer to the apex. All PSMs whose mass shifts fall within these boundaries are assigned to that peak, forming the **mass‑shift bin** that subsequent localization, spectral similarity, and RT analyses operate on. Peaks are then iteratively annotated (using Unimod, isotopic patterns, and user‑specified masses) by exact matches within a narrow tolerance (e.g. ≤0.01 Da) or by decomposing the observed delta into up to two known modifications. [storage.prod.researchhub](https://storage.prod.researchhub.com/uploads/papers/2023/10/25/2020.07.08.192583.full.pdf)

## Per‑position rescoring for localization

For each mass‑shift peak, PTM‑Shepherd constructs a localization profile by re‑scoring every PSM in that peak with the modification placed at every possible position in the peptide. The core idea is: if adding the mass shift at a particular site increases the number of matched fragment ions relative to the unmodified scoring, that site is a plausible localization for the mass shift. [ptmshepherd.nesvilab](https://ptmshepherd.nesvilab.org)

Operationally, PTM‑Shepherd uses the same scoring function as MSFragger: for each PSM, it systematically places the mass shift at each residue (and possible termini), regenerates theoretical fragments, and recomputes the match score against the original spectrum. A PSM is deemed “localizable” if there exists at least one position where adding the mass shift yields more matched fragment ions than using only unshifted fragment ions (i.e. no explicit modification). When multiple positions produce equally optimal scores (e.g. multiple serines with identical fragment support), PTM‑Shepherd distributes the “localization weight” across those residues rather than choosing one arbitrarily. [storage.prod.researchhub](https://storage.prod.researchhub.com/uploads/papers/2023/10/25/2020.07.08.192583.full.pdf)

Across all localizable PSMs in a peak, it counts how often the mass shift is localized to each amino acid or terminus and normalizes by the background occurrence of that residue in all localizable PSMs. This yields a **localization propensity profile**: a normalized enrichment (or depletion) of the mass shift on each residue and at the N‑terminus. Peaks with strong enrichment on specific residues (e.g. lysine for +42.01 Da, cysteine for +57.02 Da) are more consistent with true covalent PTMs, whereas peaks with flat or very weak localization patterns often indicate non‑specific interactions, solution adducts, or instrument/processing artifacts. [biorxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2022/09/13/2022.09.12.507594/DC2/embed/media-2.docx?download=true)

## Spectral similarity and RT shift for validating PTMs

Once peaks and localizations are established, PTM‑Shepherd compares modified peptides to their unmodified counterparts to quantify spectral and RT effects. For each peptide sequence and charge state, PTM‑Shepherd aggregates PSMs with negligible mass shift (e.g. <0.001 Da) as the **unmodified reference set**, randomly subsampling to at most ~50 spectra per peptide to control computation. Each mass‑shifted PSM is then compared to its peptide‑ and charge‑matched unmodified spectra via a cosine similarity over fragment intensities; these comparisons are confined within the same LC‑MS run to avoid cross‑run differences. The per‑peptide similarity scores are averaged and then aggregated across all peptides assigned to a peak to yield that peak’s **spectral similarity profile**. [biorxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2022/09/13/2022.09.12.507594/DC2/embed/media-2.docx?download=true)

In parallel, PTM‑Shepherd computes the RT shift of modified versus unmodified peptides. For each mass‑shifted PSM, it takes the average difference in retention time relative to all corresponding unmodified PSMs of the same sequence and charge within the same run. These differences are averaged at the peptide level, then across all peptides in the peak, providing a **mean RT shift** (and optionally variance) for that mass‑shift. Peaks representing true chemical modifications often show coherent RT shifts (e.g. hydrophobicity changes) and characteristic spectral similarity patterns, while random matches, isotopic artifacts, or loose adducts tend to exhibit high spectral similarity (spectra nearly identical) with minimal RT change or inconsistent behavior across peptides. [biorxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.03.29.534843.full)

PTM‑Shepherd also reports related metrics such as log2 fold‑change of intensity between shifted and unshifted peptides, localized PSM counts, and a “peak_signal” quality score; sample‑prep artifacts often show reduced intensity relative to unmodified peptides and lower‑quality peaks. Together, localization enrichment, spectral similarity, RT shift, intensity changes, and peak quality provide a multi‑dimensional view that helps distinguish genuine PTMs from artifacts/adducts. [biorxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2022/09/13/2022.09.12.507594/DC2/embed/media-2.docx?download=true)

## Formaldehyde (+30.01 Da) adduct example

In their FFPE (formalin‑fixed paraffin‑embedded) analyses, Nesvizhskii and colleagues highlight the formaldehyde adduct (+30.01 Da, annotated as “methylol” in Unimod) as a characteristic mass shift that PTM‑Shepherd can profile. This +30.01 Da peak appears among the top mass‑shift peaks in several FFPE datasets, reflecting extensive exposure to formaldehyde during fixation. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33568339/)

PTM‑Shepherd finds that the +30.01 Da mass shift **lacks significant localization**: it is localized less than 10% of the time and shows no strong enrichment for any particular amino acid, which contrasts with canonical PTMs that show clear residue specificity (e.g. lysine‑targeted modifications). This weak localization, combined with the absence of pronounced RT shifts or distinctive spectral changes across peptides, supports the interpretation that formaldehyde in these samples predominantly forms non‑covalent adducts or reversible interactions that survive into MS analysis, rather than stable covalent PTMs. The formaldehyde example thus illustrates how a high‑intensity mass‑shift peak can be confidently classified as an artifact/adduct by integrating localization, RT, and spectral‑similarity metrics rather than by mass alone. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33568339/)
